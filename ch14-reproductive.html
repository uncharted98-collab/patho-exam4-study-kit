Before the Test Cheat Sheet
  • ★ Hormone chain: Hypothalamus makes GnRH → anterior pituitary makes FSH + LH → ovaries or testes make sex hormones (, ).
  • ★ LH surge at the peak of estrogen = ovulation (oocyte breaks out of the follicle).
  • ★ Follicular phase = estrogen rules. Luteal phase = corpus luteum makes lots of progesterone + estrogens (, ).
  • ★ No pregnancy → progesterone and estrogen drop about 14 days after ovulation → menstruation.
  • ★ hCG comes from the zygote 2–3 days after implantation; it keeps the corpus luteum working. Home pregnancy tests detect hCG in urine.
  • ★ Progesterone causes pregnancy nausea, headaches, constipation, indigestion, swelling, and raises body temp slightly (practice Q4).
  • ★ Male: LH → Leydig (interstitial endocrine) cells → testosterone. FSH → Sertoli (nurse) cells → inhibin + help make sperm.
  • ★ Sperm need a scrotum 2–3 degrees cooler than core. Hot tubs and cryptorchidism hurt sperm making, not testosterone (practice Q6).
  • ★ Infertility = no pregnancy after 1 year of regular unprotected sex; affects 10–15% of couples.
  • ★ 3 causes of infertility: hormone imbalance, motility impairment (adhesions, blockage, leiomyomas, thick mucus), immune problems (antibodies to sperm). Leiomyomas = motility impairment (practice Q15).
  • ★ Amenorrhea can come from emotional stress or prolonged hard exercise (practice Q5).
  • ★ PID = ascending STI (Chlamydia, N. gonorrhoeae). Minimum signs: lower abdominal tenderness, adnexal tenderness, cervical motion tenderness. Treat partners too.
  • ★ PCOS = too many androgens, high LH, anovulation, insulin resistance. Rotterdam: 2 of 3 (androgen excess, ovulatory dysfunction, polycystic ovaries).
  • ★ Ovarian cancer is "silent" — 3 of 4 women already have spread at diagnosis. CA-125 is for monitoring treatment and recurrence, not prognosis at diagnosis.
  • ★ Menopause = 12 months without a period; FSH rises (best marker). Do not start HRT after age 65 (dementia risk). Nonhormone tips: layers, no spicy food/caffeine/alcohol, relaxation, deep breathing (practice Q1).
  • ★ ED: erection needs parasympathetic S2–S4 signals and trapped blood in the corpora cavernosa. Sildenafil, vardenafil, tadalafil relax smooth muscle. Never with nitrates Ⓜ Merck.
  • ★ BPH is benign, grows near the urethra (periurethral) → urinary symptoms early. Benign prostate feels soft; cancer feels hard, uneven, cobblestone. Acute urinary retention is an emergency.
  • ★ Prostate cancer starts in the peripheral gland → no urinary symptoms until late. Gleason score 2–10; lower = better. Bone is the most common distant site.
  • ★ Testicular cancer: age 20–40, small painless lump. Big risk: cryptorchidism (20–40× more likely). No transscrotal biopsy — diagnosis is by radical orchiectomy. Monthly self-exam.
  • ★ Testosterone deficiency → thinner skin, clear skin (less acne), less hair, less muscle (practice Q3).

How it normally works

Normal anatomy and physiology, the feedback loops, and the step-by-step sequences.

Anatomy

Female

  • External genitalia (): mons pubis, labia majora, vestibule, Bartholin glands (greater vestibular), labia minora, clitoris (like the penis; swells with arousal), Skene glands (lesser vestibular, at urethral opening), vaginal opening, perineum. Job: protection and lubrication.
  • Vagina: outlet for menstrual fluid, entry for penis and sperm, lower birth canal ().
  • Cervix: inlet to the uterus; its mucus protects from infection and changes how welcoming it is to sperm.
  • Uterus: 3 layers — endometrium (inner, builds and sheds), myometrium (muscle, contracts for menses and birth), perimetrium (outer).
  • Uterine (fallopian) tubes: have fimbriae (finger-like fringe) that sweep the egg in; meeting place for egg and sperm ().
  • Ovaries: almond-shaped; make oocytes, estrogens, progesterone, inhibin. Thecal endocrine cells make estrogens; corpus luteum makes estrogens + progesterone.

Male ()

  • Testes: seminiferous tubules (sperm made here; Sertoli/nurse cells); Leydig/interstitial endocrine cells make testosterone.
  • Epididymis (sperm reservoir) → vas (ductus) deferens → urethra.
  • Accessory glands: seminal vesicles, prostate (walnut size, below bladder, wraps the urethra; makes PSA and prostatic acid phosphatase; fluid boosts sperm motility and lowers vaginal acidity), bulbourethral (Cowper) glands.
  • Penis: glans penis, prepuce (foreskin), corpora cavernosa and corpus spongiosum (erectile tissue).
  • Sperm (): head with acrosome cap, neck, midpiece with mitochondria (energy), tail/flagellum (swims).
Physiology (normal, step by step)

F1. Ovarian (ovulatory) cycle — , 14.5, 14.6

  1. Hypothalamus releases GnRH in a monthly pattern.
  2. Anterior pituitary releases FSH and LH.
  3. About 10 primary (inactive) follicles become secondary (active) follicles.
  4. Follicular phase: secondary follicles grow and make estrogens.
  5. One follicle becomes dominant (makes lots of estrogen). Others die by apoptosis = follicle atresia.
  6. High estrogen tells the pituitary to lower FSH.
  7. LH stays up, then surges at peak estrogen → oocyte bursts out = ovulation.
  8. Luteal phase: ruptured follicle becomes the corpus luteum → lots of progesterone + estrogens.
  9. Pregnancy: hCG keeps corpus luteum going until the placenta takes over (about 14 weeks).
  10. No pregnancy: corpus luteum breaks down; hormones drop within ~14 days → menses.

F2. Endometrial (uterine) cycle

  1. Proliferative (end of menses → ovulation): estrogen grows the lining 6–8× thicker.
  2. Secretory (ovulation → menses): progesterone + estrogen make it thick, vascular, swollen, ready for implantation.
  3. Menstrual: corpus luteum dies → estrogen and progesterone stop → surface layer sheds.

F3. Hormone actions

  • Estrogens (E1 estrone, E2 estradiol, E3 estriol): organ development, fat contour, breast and bone growth at puberty, ovulation, pregnancy/lactation support, thin watery sperm-friendly mucus, axillary/pubic hair, skin maintenance, less bone resorption, sodium and water retention.
  • Progesterone: thickens lining, nourishes early embryo, relaxes uterine smooth muscle (keeps embryo in), raises core temp slightly; causes nausea, headaches, constipation, indigestion, swelling in pregnancy.
  • hCG (from zygote), prolactin (anterior pituitary; milk production), oxytocin (posterior pituitary; labor contractions and milk ejection).
  • Testosterone: organ development, sperm production, protein metabolism, muscle mass, skin thickness, oil glands, pubic/chest/facial hair, deeper voice.

F4. Male hormone axis and spermatogenesis ()

  1. Hypothalamus → GnRH.
  2. Anterior pituitary → LH + FSH (fairly constant all adult life, not cyclic).
  3. LH → Leydig cells → testosterone.
  4. FSH → Sertoli (nurse) cells → inhibin + support sperm making.
  5. Sperm form in seminiferous tubules (needs cooler scrotum) and mature in about 60 days.
  6. Path: tubules → epididymis → vas deferens → urethra → out.

F5. Erection ()

Arousal → parasympathetic S2–S4 → more blood in → smooth muscle relaxes, veins pinched → blood trapped in corpora cavernosa. Ⓜ Merck nitric oxide → cGMP → relaxation.

Positive and Negative Feedback Loops

J1. Negative feedback — female ()

Trigger: estrogen/progesterone/inhibin levels rise → Sensor: hypothalamus and anterior pituitary → Control center: hypothalamus (GnRH) and pituitary → Effector: less FSH and LH released → Result: ovarian hormone output steadies.

J2. Positive feedback — LH surge

Trigger: dominant follicle's estrogen reaches its peak → Sensor: pituitary → Control center: pituitary → Effector: big LH surge → Result: ovulation (loop ends when follicle ruptures). Note: the file describes the surge at peak estrogen; it does not use the words "positive feedback."

J3. Negative feedback — male testosterone ()

Trigger: testosterone too high → Sensor: hypothalamus and pituitary → Control center: hypothalamus/pituitary → Effector: less GnRH and LH → Result: testosterone falls back to normal.

J4. Negative feedback — inhibin

Trigger: FSH/LH rise → Sensor/effector: Sertoli cells make inhibin → Control center: pituitary → Result: gonadotropins suppressed.

J5. Broken loop — menopause

Trigger: ovaries stop making hormones → Sensor: hypothalamus sees low hormones → Control: more GnRH → Effector: pituitary makes more FSH/LH → Result: no response from ovaries → FSH stays high (the lab marker).

J6. Vicious loop — PCOS

Trigger: no ovulation → constant estrogen → Control: altered GnRH → Effector: high LH, low FSH → Result: more androgens, more immature follicles → still no ovulation.

J7. Positive feedback — oxytocin in labor

The file says oxytocin stimulates labor contractions and milk ejection. The full loop steps are Not given in this file or Merck (pages read).

Step-by-Step / Sequential Order

The sequences (reference)

  1. Sperm path: seminiferous tubules → epididymis → vas deferens → urethra → vagina → cervix → uterus → uterine tube.
  2. Egg path: follicle → ovulation → fimbriae sweep → uterine tube → meets sperm → zygote → implants in endometrium.
  3. Follicle stages: primordial → primary (inactive) → secondary (active) → dominant → ruptured → corpus luteum → degraded (or atretic if it never matured) ().
  4. Germ cell → sperm: germ cell (mitosis) → spermatogonium → spermatocyte → spermatozoon.
  5. PID spread: vagina → cervix → uterus → tubes → ovaries → peritoneum.
  6. Ovarian cancer spread: ovary surface → shed into peritoneum → implant → invade bowel/bladder → pelvic nodes → distant.
  7. Prostate cancer progression: proliferative inflammatory atrophy → PIN → adenocarcinoma → capsule → nodes → bone.
  8. Testicular cancer: fetal mutation → puberty hormones → carcinoma in situ → invasive carcinoma.
  9. Infertility work-up: history/physical → semen analysis + ovulation check → hysterosalpingography → laparoscopy → treat cause.
  10. Amenorrhea evaluation (): hCG (pregnancy?) → PRL and TSH → FSH → imaging (MRI if CNS cause). Exact branch order should be confirmed against .
  11. Gleason score: biopsy → grade most common pattern (1–5) → grade second pattern (1–5) → add → 2–10.
  12. BPH treatment ladder: watch → drugs → laser/heat → TURP → stent if not a candidate.
Genetic Factors
  • BRCA1 (chromosome 17q21): ovarian and breast–ovarian cancer; risk 15–45% (file). Ⓜ Merck 20–40% lifetime ovarian risk.
  • BRCA2 (13q14): some inherited ovarian and breast cancer. Ⓜ Merck 11–20%. Both autosomal dominant.
  • TP53 mutations in almost all high-grade serous ovarian cancer.
  • Ovarian cancer: 5–10% hereditary; patterns: ovarian alone, breast–ovarian, ovarian–colon.
  • HPC1 at chromosome 1q24: hereditary prostate cancer → younger age, higher grade, more advanced stage. 5–10% of prostate cancer inherited.
  • Testicular cancer: possible chromosome 14 alteration; mutation likely during fetal development.
  • PCOS: more likely with an affected mother or sister.
  • Ⓜ Merck prostate cancer risk with BRCA1/2 and Lynch syndrome.

Making Connections
  • Endocrine chapter / negative feedback: GnRH-FSH/LH-sex hormone loop is a classic negative feedback axis.
  • Cellular proliferation and cancer (neoplasia): ovarian, prostate, testicular cancers follow initiator/promoter steps and TNM staging.
  • Inflammation and immunity: PID shows hyperemia, edema, exudate, scarring; immune infertility = antibodies to sperm.
  • Diabetes / metabolism: PCOS insulin resistance, acanthosis nigricans; metformin crosses over.
  • Cardiovascular: menopause raises LDL:HDL; ED shares atherosclerosis; Ⓜ Merck ED can be first sign of heart disease.
  • Musculoskeletal: menopause bone loss (osteoclasts vs osteoblasts); raloxifene.
  • Renal/urinary: BPH obstruction → retention → renal failure.
  • Neurology: spinal cord injury at S2–S4 → ED; pituitary adenoma → hyperprolactinemia.
  • Thyroid: TSH checked in amenorrhea () and ED work-ups.
  • Mental health: stress → amenorrhea; depression ↔ ED and menopause.
  • Case 14.2 (endometrial cancer): unopposed estrogen thickens endometrium → cancer — ties HRT rules to cell growth.
  • Pharmacology: PDE5 inhibitors + nitrates = dangerous hypotension Ⓜ Merck.

Infertility (general altered reproductive function) LO3, LO4
Pathophysiology (step by step)

  1. Pregnancy needs every step to work: ovulation, sperm making, intercourse, ejaculation, fertilization, implantation, embryo growth.
  2. A problem at any one step breaks the chain.
  3. Hormone imbalance → no ovulation or rare ovulation; low testosterone → fewer sperm.
  4. Motility impairment → adhesions or blockage from cervix to ovary stop egg and sperm from meeting ().
  5. Immune problem → antibodies destroy sperm before they reach the egg.
Etiology

Male, female, or both partners. Often no exact cause is found. Examples: anovulation, hyperprolactinemia from a pituitary adenoma, infections (PID), leiomyomas (fibroids), endometriosis, thick or hostile cervical mucus, varicocele (Case Study 14.1).

Signs

Irregular periods or amenorrhea on history; pelvic findings; in males often no signs. Symptoms: Cannot get pregnant; may have pelvic pain, dysmenorrhea, dyspareunia, testicular/scrotal pain.

Expected treatment

Supportive counseling; teaching about intercourse frequency; avoid lubricants (can act as spermicide); basic health. Treat infections fast and fully. Surgery for leiomyomas, endometriosis, adhesions. Prognosis

Not given as a number in this file. Ⓜ Merck clomiphene leads to ovulation in 75–80% of women, but pregnancy rate is at most 40–50%.

Nursing interventions

  • Use a private, calm space and open questions. Rationale: Infertility is "highly complex and emotionally charged."
  • Teach both partners together when possible. Rationale: Either or both partners can be the cause.
  • Teach BBT charting and ovulation kit use. Rationale: Helps time intercourse and confirm ovulation.
  • Teach to avoid lubricants and scrotal heat (hot tubs). Rationale: Lubricants can kill sperm; heat stops sperm making.
  • Screen for STI symptoms and urge early care. Rationale: Pelvic infection is a top cause of tubal scarring.

Endometriosis LO3
Pathophysiology

1) Endometrial tissue grows outside the uterus (, red-blue nodules on the ovary). 2) It reacts to hormones. 3) This causes pelvic adhesions. 4) Pelvic structures get distorted. 5) Result: dysmenorrhea, pelvic pain, and infertility. Merck theories: retrograde menstruation through the tubes, coelomic metaplasia, Müllerian rests, spread through lymph or blood Ⓜ Merck.

Etiology

File does not give a cause. Merck theories above Ⓜ Merck.

Signs

Red-blue nodules seen on laparoscopy; Ⓜ Merck ovarian cyst consistent with an endometrioma on ultrasound or MRI. Symptoms: Dysmenorrhea, dyspareunia, pelvic pain; Ⓜ Merck cyclic midline pelvic pain before or during menses; new painful periods after years of easy periods is a clue Ⓜ Merck.

Diagnostics

Laparoscopy (views structures outside uterus). Ⓜ Merck diagnosis is often clinical; laparoscopy with biopsy confirms; transvaginal ultrasound or MRI can support Ⓜ Merck. CA-125 can be elevated in endometriosis (file).

Risk

Infertility; adhesions. Treatment: File: surgery may be needed. Ⓜ Merck NSAIDs for pain; continuous combined contraceptives; progestins; GnRH agonists (leuprolide) limited to 6 months or less due to bone loss; GnRH antagonists (elagolix, relugolix); surgery to remove implants; hysterectomy ± bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy is definitive Ⓜ Merck.

Prognosis

Not given in this file or Merck (pages read).

Nursing interventions

Pain scale with each period (tracks pattern); teach NSAID timing as ordered (Ⓜ Merck NSAIDs relieve pain); teach bone health if on GnRH drugs (Ⓜ Merck bone loss risk); offer fertility counseling referral (infertility link).

Merck pages: Endometriosis.

Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID) LO3, LO4, LO5
Pathophysiology (Fig 14.14)

  1. Sexual intercourse pushes germs into the vagina.
  2. Infection climbs up: cervix → uterus → uterine tubes → ovaries → even the peritoneal cavity.
  3. Germs attach to the epithelial cells lining the tract.
  4. Acute inflammation and immune response start.
  5. Tissue becomes hyperemic (too much blood, red) and edematous (swollen).
  6. Tubes fill with purulent exudate (pus) and get blocked.
  7. Repeated or chronic infection → scar tissue, worst in tubes and ovaries.
  8. Scars block ovulation and egg travel → ectopic pregnancy, tubo-ovarian abscess, adhesions, infertility.
Etiology

STI, most often Chlamydia trachomatis + Neisseria gonorrhoeae. Merck adds Mycoplasma genitalium and vaginal bacteria including those of bacterial vaginosis Ⓜ Merck. In males, STIs cause similar problems in the urethra, epididymis, testes.

Signs

Purulent cervical discharge, cervical motion tenderness, adnexal tenderness, lower abdominal tenderness, fever, cervicitis. Ⓜ Merck friable cervix (bleeds easily); a palpable adnexal mass suggests tubo-ovarian abscess Ⓜ Merck. Symptoms: Pelvic and lower abdominal pain, malaise. Early infection is often silent → spreads to others.

Risk to develop (Merck)

age under 25, first sex before 15, new partner in past 12 months or 2 or more prior partners, recent discharge or pelvic pain, being single, low socioeconomic status Ⓜ Merck. Complications: ectopic pregnancy, tubo-ovarian abscess, pelvic adhesions, infertility; Merck adds Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome (perihepatitis with right upper quadrant pain) Ⓜ Merck.

Treatment

Immediate and often long course of oral or IV antibiotics. ★ Hospitalize with IV antibiotics and fluids if: pregnant; immunodeficient; severe illness, nausea, vomiting, or high fever; can't follow or tolerate oral treatment; may need emergency surgery (e.g., appendicitis); tubo-ovarian abscess. Treat partners. No intercourse until patient and partners are treated and cured. Ⓜ Merck if no response to oral therapy in 72 hours, reevaluate and give IV therapy.

Prognosis

Goal is preventing complications. Repeated infection raises scarring and infertility risk.

Nursing interventions

  • Give antibiotics on time and finish the full course. Rationale: "Treated quickly and completely" prevents scarring.
  • Monitor temp, pain, and abdominal tenderness. Rationale: Worsening suggests abscess or failure of oral therapy.
  • Teach abstinence until all partners are cured. Rationale: Prevents reinfection.
  • Teach condom use and STI screening. Rationale: Early infection is silent.
  • Teach ectopic pregnancy warning signs for future pregnancies. Rationale: Tubal scarring raises ectopic risk.

Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) LO3, LO4, LO5
Overview

Merck now calls it Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS), formerly PCOS, to stress hormone and metabolic risks Ⓜ Merck.

Pathophysiology (Fig 14.15)

  1. Pituitary secretes high LH.
  2. Ovaries make excess androgens.
  3. FSH is still present, so follicles start to grow, but they never mature.
  4. No ovulation → no corpus luteum → low progesterone; estrogen is constant (acyclic).
  5. Estrogen alters GnRH → even higher LH → more androgens, FSH suppressed (a vicious cycle).
  6. Chronic anovulation → both ovaries become enlarged and full of cysts ().
  7. Insulin resistance: extra insulin pushes ovaries to make even more androgens.
  8. Androgens in fat convert to estrogen → obesity link.
Etiology

Unknown. Genetic link: more likely if mother or sister has PCOS. Ⓜ Merck possible abnormal cytochrome P450c17 enzyme raising androgen production.

Signs

Hirsutism (), acne, male-pattern baldness, obesity (about half), acanthosis nigricans (dark velvety skin on neck, armpits, inner thighs, vulva, under breasts), hypertension, enlarged cystic ovaries on ultrasound. Symptoms: Irregular or prolonged periods, cycles longer than 35 days or fewer than 8 per year, infertility; Merck adds fatigue, mood swings, depression, anxiety, sleep problems Ⓜ Merck.

Diagnostics

★ Rotterdam criteria: 2 of 3 — androgen excess, ovulatory dysfunction, polycystic ovaries. Labs to find hormone changes and rule out hyperprolactinemia and ovarian/adrenal tumors. Transvaginal ultrasound. Fasting cholesterol and triglycerides. Glucose tolerance test (insulin resistance). Ⓜ Merck polycystic ovaries on ultrasound = 12 or more follicles per ovary 2–9 mm, and/or ovarian volume over 10 mL; labs include testosterone, DHEAS, prolactin, TSH, FSH Ⓜ Merck.

Risk

Long-term cardiovascular risk, diabetes, hypertension, obstructive sleep apnea. Merck adds endometrial hyperplasia and endometrial cancer, fatty liver disease, depression, eating disorders Ⓜ Merck.

Treatment

Want pregnancy: clomiphene citrate (watch ovarian hyperstimulation); metformin (often with clomiphene); injectable FSH and LH if needed; laparoscopic laser puncture of follicles. Not wanting pregnancy: low-dose birth control pills; progesterone 10–14 days a month (regulates periods, not androgens); spironolactone for hair and acne; hair removal methods; manage weight, lipids, diabetes, BP. Ⓜ Merck lifestyle and weight loss; letrozole may work better than clomiphene in women with obesity and PCOS; eflornithine cream for facial hair; spironolactone needs effective contraception because it may cause birth defects Ⓜ Merck.

Prognosis

Chronic condition; outcome depends on managing metabolic risks. Not given as a number.

Nursing interventions

Weigh and measure BP each visit (cardiometabolic risk); teach diet and activity (weight loss improves insulin resistance, Merck); screen mood and body image (Ⓜ Merck depression, low self-esteem); teach contraception with spironolactone (teratogenic risk, Merck); teach signs of ovarian hyperstimulation with fertility drugs (file caution).

Merck pages: Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS); Ovulatory Dysfunction.

Ovarian Cancer LO3, LO4, LO5
Pathophysiology

  1. Genes + environment combine.
  2. Each ovulation breaks the ovary surface → inflammation → repair. More ovulations over life = more "wear and tear" = more risk.
  3. Mutations (BRCA1, BRCA2, TP53) let cells grow out of control.
  4. Tumor types: epithelial (surface; serous adenocarcinoma most common), germ cell (25%; mostly benign in adults, more malignant in children/young adults), sex cord (10%).
  5. Spread: cells shed into the peritoneal cavity → seed and implant on the peritoneum → invade bowel and bladder → pelvic lymph nodes.
  6. The open peritoneum lets tumors grow big without blocking organs → few early symptoms → late diagnosis ().
Staging (file)

I = one or both ovaries; II = into pelvis; III = peritoneum outside pelvis; IV = distant. SEER groups: localized, regional, distant.

Etiology

5–10% hereditary (ovarian alone, breast–ovarian, ovarian–colon patterns). BRCA1 (chromosome 17q21) risk 15–45%; BRCA2 (13q14); both autosomal dominant. Merck says: BRCA1 lifetime ovarian cancer risk 20–40%, BRCA2 11–20% Ⓜ Merck.

Signs

Abdominal distention, enlarged ovary on bimanual exam, ascites Ⓜ Merck. Symptoms: Often none early; vague bloating; pressure or pain with large tumors. Ⓜ Merck dyspepsia, early satiety, change in bowel habits, urinary frequency.

Diagnostics

History, bimanual pelvic exam, laparoscopy or laparotomy, cytology of ovarian epithelial cells, CA-125. Ⓜ Merck ultrasound first if early cancer suspected; CT or MRI if advanced; CA-125 elevated in 80% of advanced epithelial cancers but may be normal early; germ cell markers include beta-hCG, LDH, alpha-fetoprotein, inhibin Ⓜ Merck.

Risk to develop

First-degree relative (mother, daughter, sister); 2 or more relatives raises risk more; BRCA; breast cancer history; many ovulations; postmenopausal hormone therapy; age (over 48% of cases over 65). Protective: pregnancy, contraceptives (anything that stops ovulation). Merck adds nulliparity, early menarche, late menopause Ⓜ Merck. Complications: Bowel/bladder invasion, metastasis.

Treatment

By stage. Stage I–II well/moderately differentiated: remove uterus, both ovaries, omentum; biopsy diaphragm and lymph nodes. Debulking = remove as much tumor as possible; optimal debulked (no visible tumor) has a better prognosis than suboptimally debulked. Fertility-sparing option for stage I well-differentiated. Advanced/less-differentiated: surgery + radiation + combination chemotherapy. Ⓜ Merck standard chemo is paclitaxel + carboplatin; PARP inhibitors as maintenance Ⓜ Merck.

Prognosis

Curable if found early; but 3 of 4 have spread at diagnosis; more than half stage III or higher. Merck 5-year survival: stage I 85–95%, II 70–78%, III 40–60%, IV 15–20% Ⓜ Merck. Ⓜ Merck no screening test exists; CA-125 is not a screening test for average-risk women Ⓜ Merck.

Nursing interventions

Take a careful family history (hereditary risk); teach to report persistent bloating (vague early sign); post-op monitoring for bleeding and infection (major surgery); chemo safety and neutropenic precautions (chemo lowers immunity); emotional support and referral for genetic counseling (BRCA affects relatives).

Menopause LO2, LO3, LO4, LO5
Pathophysiology

  1. Ovaries age; follicles run out.
  2. Ovaries can't answer FSH/LH anymore.
  3. Low ovarian hormones → hypothalamus makes more GnRH.
  4. Pituitary makes more FSH and LH (negative feedback is lost) — but it doesn't help.
  5. Low estrogen → target tissues atrophy (shrink): vagina, uterus, ovaries, breasts, bladder, skin.
  6. Bone: osteoclasts (bone breakers) live longer; osteoblasts (bone builders) die sooner → bone loss.
Etiology

Normal biologic stage, about age 48–55. Perimenopause = 4–5-year transition; first sign is subtle bleeding changes. Ⓜ Merck average age in the US is 51.

Signs

No periods for 12 months, reddened skin and sweating with hot flashes, thin vaginal walls, smaller breasts, less skin elasticity, bone demineralization, LDL:HDL ratio up. Symptoms: Hot flashes (30 seconds to 5 minutes), breast tenderness, moodiness, irritability, anxiety, vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, sleep disruption, fatigue, low libido, urinary urgency and stress incontinence ().

Diagnostics

★ Amenorrhea for at least 12 months with no other cause. No single test. FSH rises (most significant marker); FSH rises higher than LH; estradiol and inhibin fall. Ⓜ Merck FSH rarely needed; a single level can mislead because it fluctuates Ⓜ Merck.

Risk

Osteoporosis, cardiovascular disease (stroke, heart attack), depression (higher if prior depression).

Treatment

Individualized. HRT: estrogen, progesterone, or both — only for severe symptoms, short term, lowest effective dose, topical when possible. Do not start HRT over age 65 (dementia). Estrogen alone raised endometrial cancer risk. Raloxifene (SERM) to prevent osteoporosis. Nonhormone measures (see Y). Ⓜ Merck add a progestogen if the woman has a uterus; risks include breast cancer, VTE, stroke, CAD, gallbladder disease, dementia; avoid starting at over 60 or 10–20 years past menopause; nonhormone drug options include SSRIs, SNRIs, gabapentin, oxybutynin, neurokinin receptor antagonists Ⓜ Merck. Merck says the start-age cutoff is over 60 (file says over 65).

Prognosis

Expected life stage; symptoms last 6 months to over 10 years Ⓜ Merck.

Nursing interventions

Teach hot flash measures (reduce triggers); teach pelvic floor exercises (stress incontinence); teach water-based lubricant (vaginal atrophy); teach fall and bone safety, weight-bearing activity (bone loss); screen mood and sleep (depression risk); report any vaginal bleeding after menopause (Case 14.2: endometrial cancer).

Erectile Dysfunction (ED) LO3, LO4
Pathophysiology (Fig 14.20)

1) Arousal. 2) Parasympathetic nerves at S2–S4 fire. 3) Blood flow to the penis rises. 4) Smooth muscle relaxes and squeezes the veins shut (veno-occlusion). 5) Blood is trapped in the corpora cavernosa → erection. Ⓜ Merck nerves release nitric oxide → more cGMP → smooth muscle relaxes → more blood flows in; PDE5 breaks down cGMP Ⓜ Merck. Any break in nerves, hormones, mind, or blood vessels → ED.

Etiology

Hormonal (hypogonadism, hypothyroidism, adrenal problems); neurologic (spinal cord or perineal nerve injury); psychological (anxiety, low self-esteem, depression); vascular (hypertension, atherosclerosis, smoking); poor veno-occlusion; medications (antihypertensives). Lifestyle: obesity, smoking, alcohol (research note).

Signs

Hypertension, obesity, low testosterone on labs. Symptoms: Cannot get or keep an erection.

Diagnostics

History and physical; testosterone, LH, thyroid hormone, prolactin. Ⓜ Merck morning testosterone; if low, check prolactin and LH; check for diabetes and lipids Ⓜ Merck. Ⓜ Merck new vascular ED can be the first sign of heart disease → cardiology referral Ⓜ Merck.

Risk

Increases with age. Treatment: Treat cause; testosterone replacement; mental health care; sildenafil, vardenafil, tadalafil; penile implants or devices; weight loss + exercise (2-year study improved sexual function). Ⓜ Merck vacuum device; intracavernosal alprostadil Ⓜ Merck.

Prognosis

Lifestyle change improved function in the study. Not given as a number.

Nursing interventions

Ask about nitrate use before PDE5 drugs (Ⓜ Merck contraindicated, dangerous hypotension); review med list for antihypertensives (can cause ED); teach smoking cessation and weight loss (research note); private, nonjudgmental talk (psychological factors); teach that an erection over 4 hours is an emergency (Ⓜ Merck priapism).

Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH) LO3, LO4, LO5
Pathophysiology

1) Aging lowers testosterone → relatively more estrogen → prostate cells multiply. 2) Testosterone turns into DHT; DHT builds up → more growth of epithelial cells, smooth muscle, and stromal fibroblasts. 3) Growth is mostly periurethral (around the urethra). 4) The urethra gets squeezed → urine can't flow well. 5) Total blockage → acute retention → renal failure if untreated. BPH is not a precursor to prostate cancer.

Etiology

Unknown; aging hormones. Over 50% of men over 60; up to 90% over 70.

Signs

Enlarged, soft prostate on DRE (); distended bladder with retention. Ⓜ Merck rubbery, nontender, may lose the median furrow. Symptoms: Frequency, dribbling, hesitancy, incontinence, urgency, retention. Merck splits these: storage (nocturia, urgency, frequency, incontinence) vs voiding (weak stream, straining, hesitancy, dribbling, incomplete emptying) Ⓜ Merck.

Diagnostics

DRE; PSA (biopsy if high, to rule out cancer); AUA Symptom Index (7 questions; total 20–35 = severe); urodynamic tests. Ⓜ Merck urinalysis and urine culture; postvoid residual by bladder ultrasound Ⓜ Merck.

Risk/complications

Acute urinary retention, renal failure, death if untreated. Ⓜ Merck stasis → stones and infection; hydronephrosis. Retention can be triggered by delaying voiding, immobility, cold, UTI, and anesthetics, anticholinergics, sympathomimetics (many OTC), opioids, alcohol Ⓜ Merck.

Treatment

Only if significant symptoms. Drugs that relax smooth muscle of arteries, prostate, bladder neck (alpha-blockers per Ⓜ Merck terazosin, doxazosin, tamsulosin, alfuzosin, silodosin). Drugs that block conversion to DHT (5-alpha-reductase inhibitors per Ⓜ Merck finasteride, dutasteride; work over months) → prostate shrinks. Laser/heat therapy. TURP (most common surgery). Urethral stent if not a candidate for others. Ⓜ Merck daily tadalafil if ED too; combination therapy beats single drug Ⓜ Merck.

Prognosis

Good with treatment; dangerous if complete obstruction ignored.

Nursing interventions

Monitor intake/output and bladder distention (retention); bladder scan for postvoid residual as ordered (Merck); review OTC cold medicines (Ⓜ Merck sympathomimetics, anticholinergics trigger retention); after TURP watch urine color and bleeding (surgical bleeding); teach not to delay voiding and limit alcohol (Merck triggers).

Prostate Cancer LO3, LO4, LO5
Pathophysiology (Fig 14.22)

1) Initiator + promoter events (genes + environment). 2) Proliferative inflammatory atrophy (hypothesized). 3) Prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia (atypical cells lining ducts) = premalignant. 4) Adenocarcinoma (over 95%) in the peripheral gland. 5) Urethra is not squeezed until late → silent early. 6) Spread to lymph nodes, then bone (most common distant site).

Etiology/risk

Age, family history, smoking, high fat and meat, low lycopene (tomato products) and fruit, high dietary calcium. 5–10% inherited; HPC1 at chromosome 1q24 → younger age, higher grade, later stage. Lifetime risk 1 in 6; 2nd leading cancer death in men; about 85% diagnosed after 65. Ⓜ Merck highest risk in Black men; BRCA1/2 and Lynch syndrome Ⓜ Merck.

Signs

Hard, asymmetric, cobblestone prostate on DRE; elevated PSA. Symptoms: Often none; later same as BPH (frequency, dribbling, hesitancy, incontinence, urgency, retention); bone pain with spread. Ⓜ Merck hematuria; spinal cord compression from bone metastasis Ⓜ Merck.

Diagnostics

Screen: DRE, transrectal ultrasound, PSA. Confirm: biopsy (fine needle, usually transrectal). Pelvic lymph node dissection; bone scan. TNM: T1 not palpable/visible; T2 confined to prostate; T3 through capsule; T4 fixed or invades nearby structures (other than seminal vesicles). Low free PSA vs total PSA → suggests cancer; high free PSA → suggests BPH or inflammation. Gleason score = most common pattern grade + second most common grade (each 1–5) → 2 to 10; most tumors 4–7 (). Ⓜ Merck PSA ≥ 4 ng/mL often prompts biopsy; free PSA under 10–20% warrants biopsy; MRI helps target biopsy Ⓜ Merck.

Complications

Metastasis; treatment effects (Merck after prostatectomy: stress incontinence, ED, urethral stricture) Ⓜ Merck.

Treatment: Radical prostatectomy (often under 70, healthy); radiation and chemo; androgen-deprivation therapy; watchful waiting for slow tumors in men without symptoms. Ⓜ Merck ADT agents include leuprolide, goserelin, degarelix, relugolix; ADT causes loss of libido, ED, hot flushes, osteoporosis, anemia, muscle loss Ⓜ Merck.

Prognosis

5-year survival over 99% local/regional; 33.5% with distant metastases. Lower Gleason = better.

Nursing interventions

Teach shared decision-making about PSA screening (Ⓜ Merck screening is controversial); post-prostatectomy catheter care and Kegel teaching (incontinence); assess bone pain and back pain with leg weakness (bone mets, cord compression); support for sexual changes (ED after surgery/ADT).

Testicular Cancer LO3, LO4, LO5
Pathophysiology (Fig 14.24)

1) A germ cell mutation happens, likely during fetal development. 2) Puberty hormones then promote growth (autonomy and anaplasia). 3) Stage 1: carcinoma in situ inside the seminiferous tubules. 4) Stage 2: invasive carcinoma. Seminomas (most common): immature spermatogonia pile up → testis enlarges and becomes fibrotic. Nonseminomas (embryonic carcinoma, yolk sac, choriocarcinoma, teratocarcinoma): look like embryonic or body tissue (skin, bone); grow faster, invade more, spread more.

Etiology

About 90% from germ cells; rest sex cord or metastases. Possible chromosome 14 change. Cryptorchidism: 20–40× more likely. Ages 20–40. Ⓜ Merck most common solid cancer in males 15–35.

Signs

Small painless testicular mass, slightly enlarged testis, enlarged scrotum. Symptoms: Heaviness, mild discomfort.

Diagnostics

Monthly self-testicular exam; physical exam; radical orchiectomy for diagnosis; no transscrotal biopsy (may seed tumor into scrotum or spread to inguinal nodes); tumor markers; ultrasound, CT, MRI. Ⓜ Merck markers are AFP, beta-hCG, LDH; pure seminoma does not raise AFP; orchiectomy is done through an inguinal approach Ⓜ Merck.

Risk/complications

Worse prognosis with bone, liver, or brain metastases; high tumor markers; primary mediastinal nonseminoma; many lung metastases. Treatment risks: infertility, secondary leukemias, kidney impairment.

Treatment

Remove tumor and testicle(s); radiation and chemo. Ⓜ Merck cisplatin-based chemo (e.g., bleomycin, etoposide, cisplatin); sperm banking before radiation or chemo Ⓜ Merck.

Prognosis

Highly curable. Seminoma without spread: 95% 5-year survival. Mediastinal nonseminoma with spread or high markers: 73%. Even widespread disease may be cured.

Nursing interventions

Teach monthly TSE (early detection); offer sperm banking talk before treatment (infertility risk, Merck); monitor kidney function on chemo (file: renal impairment); body-image support (young men, loss of a testicle).

Unfolding Case Study — "Maya, 24"

Stage 1 – Admission (ED): Maya, 24, has lower abdominal pain for 3 days, fever, and yellow vaginal discharge. New partner 2 months ago. Nurse notices: fever, guarding, pain with movement. Nurse does: vital signs, pain scale, gets urine for pregnancy test as ordered Ⓜ Merck, prepares for pelvic exam and NAAT/cultures, ESR/CRP.

Stage 2 – Change: Exam shows cervical motion, adnexal, and lower abdominal tenderness. She is vomiting and can't keep pills down. Nurse notices: she meets hospital criteria (can't tolerate oral treatment, severe illness). Nurse does: reports to provider with SBAR, starts IV access, anticipates IV antibiotics and IV fluids.

Stage 3 – Intervention: IV antibiotics on schedule. Ultrasound ordered to rule out tubo-ovarian abscess. Nurse does: monitors temp trend, I&O, pain, signs of worsening (rigid abdomen, falling BP). Teaches no intercourse; asks about partner treatment.

Stage 4 – Outcome: Fever gone; switched to oral antibiotics. Nurse does: teach-back on finishing all medicine, partner treatment, condoms, and future ectopic pregnancy warning signs (one-sided pain, missed period). Refers for STI counseling.

"What Went Wrong?" Error Spotting

Answers are blurred. Decide first, then tap to check.

  1. Scenario: A nurse tells a PID patient she can resume sex as soon as her pain is gone. Error: Patient must abstain during treatment and until partners are treated and cured. Correct action: Teach abstinence and partner treatment.

Lifespan Differences
  • Pediatric/adolescent: menarche begins cycles; amenorrhea in a teen can be from stress (practice Q5); germ cell ovarian tumors more often malignant in children and young adults; cryptorchidism is a childhood finding that raises later testicular cancer risk; PCOS can appear in teens ( shows ages 17 and 21).
  • Pregnant: hCG, prolactin, oxytocin active; progesterone side effects; pregnancy protects against ovarian cancer; pregnant PID patients must be hospitalized; clomiphene/letrozole/spironolactone not in pregnancy Ⓜ Merck.
  • Adult: infertility peak concern; PID mostly young; testicular cancer age 20–40; ED prevalence rises with age.
  • Geriatric: menopause around 48–55; HRT not started over 65; BPH in most men over 60–70; prostate cancer about 85% after 65; over 48% of ovarian cancers over 65; watchful waiting option; fall and bone risks.
Cultural and Ethical Considerations
  • Infertility is "highly complex and emotionally charged" — respect beliefs about assisted reproduction (IVF, insemination) and religious views.
  • Modesty: offer same-gender caregivers or chaperones for pelvic, testicular, and rectal exams where possible.
  • Sexual health talks need privacy, plain language, and no judgment (STIs, ED).
  • Confidentiality for adolescents seeking STI care (follow state law).
  • Partner notification balances public health and privacy.
  • Genetic testing (BRCA, HPC1): informed consent; results affect relatives.
  • Watchful waiting vs aggressive treatment in prostate cancer: respect patient values; shared decision-making Ⓜ Merck.
  • Fertility preservation (sperm banking) before cancer treatment — offer, don't assume Ⓜ Merck.
  • Body image and gender identity: hirsutism, loss of a testicle, ED can affect identity; offer support.
  • Merck notes PID risk factors include nonwhite race and low socioeconomic status, and prostate cancer risk is highest in Black men Ⓜ Merck — address access to screening without stereotyping.

Body System "Home Base"

The reproductive system's job is to keep our species going. It makes gametes (sperm and oocytes) that join to form a new person. It needs three things working together: structure (organs and open passageways), nerves (autonomic control of sexual function), and hormones (hypothalamus → pituitary → gonads). Females have a cyclic monthly pattern; males have a steady pattern. The system overlaps with the urinary system in males (prostate, urethra), the endocrine system (feedback loops), bones (estrogen protects bone), the heart and vessels (cholesterol, erections), and the mind (stress, mood). When any part fails, you get infertility, pain, abnormal periods, urinary trouble, or cancer.

The zygote shouts "hCG!" and the babysitter stays on the job.

In men, the race is steady. LH tells the Leydig cells to make testosterone. FSH tells the Sertoli cells to make inhibin and help sperm grow. And sperm like it cool — two to three degrees cooler than the body. That's why Dave in the practice question should skip the hot tub.
