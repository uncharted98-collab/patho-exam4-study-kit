Degenerative Changes in Aging

Why bodies age, how bone and brain break down, and how a nurse keeps older adults safe.

Before the Test Cheat Sheet
  1. ★ Developmental theories = genes run aging. Stochastic theories = random damage piles up. (Practice Q1 answer: developmental.)
  2. ★ Aging body: less muscle, more fat, so LESS total body water. Higher risk of high or low sodium. (Practice Q2.)
  3. ★ Immune senescence: weaker T-cell function (same T-cell number), MORE autoimmune activity, LESS IgE allergy. Thymus shrinks to 15% of peak after age 50. (Practice Q3.)
  4. ★ Aging brain: fewer neurons, less myelin, slower nerve conduction, bigger ventricles, lipofuscin. (Practice Q4.)
  5. ★ Osteoporosis = osteoclasts (bone eaters) beat osteoblasts (bone builders). (Practice Q5, Q7.)
  6. ★ Poor wound healing: thin skin, lost fat under skin, weaker collagen, atrophied capillaries supporting the dermis. (Practice Q6.)
  7. ★ T score: normal more than −1.0; osteopenia −1.0 to −2.5; osteoporosis less than −2.5. Z score less than −1.5 hints at a secondary cause.
  8. ★ Central DEXA (hip + lumbar spine) is the main bone density test. Heel ultrasound (QUS) is cheaper but less accurate.
  9. Calcium 1,200 mg/day (postmenopausal women, older men), in divided doses; vitamin D 400–600 IU/day (file's numbers).
  10. ★ Teriparatide (a PTH form) is the anabolic drug: it BUILDS bone. Most others are antiresorptive (slow bone loss).
  11. ★ Alzheimer = senile plaques (amyloid beta, OUTSIDE neurons) + neurofibrillary tangles (tau, INSIDE neurons). Dementia severity tracks with the tangles.
  12. ★ Alzheimer memory loss = hippocampus. (Practice Q10.)
  13. ★ Alzheimer is diagnosed for certain only at autopsy. MRI/CT rule out other causes.
  14. ★ Alzheimer drugs: cholinesterase inhibitors raise acetylcholine.
  15. ★ Progeria (HGPS): kids age fast; death most often from atherosclerosis. Clue: high hyaluronic acid. (Practice Q8, Q9.)
  16. Life span is fixed near 100 years. Life expectancy = age when 50% of a group is still alive.
  17. Older adults: hard to cool off (fewer sweat glands) and easy to get too cold.
  18. Kidneys: lower GFR, so drugs build up and toxicity rises.

How it normally works

Normal anatomy and physiology, the feedback loops, and the step-by-step sequences.

Anatomy
  • Spongy (trabecular) bone: mesh of support beams (trabeculae). Found at the ends of long bones (epiphyses), in vertebrae, ribs, skull. Lost first in osteoporosis.
  • Compact (cortical) bone: dense outer shell; mostly in the shafts (diaphyses) of long bones, so mostly in the arms and legs (appendicular skeleton).
  • Thoracic spine: has a normal gentle outward curve (kyphosis). Too much = hyperkyphosis ().
  • Chromosome ends: each chromatid is capped by a telomere ().
  • Thymus: makes T cells mature; shrinks to 15% of peak size after age 50.
  • Hippocampus and medial temporal lobe: memory centers; first hit by AD tangles. Cerebral cortex: thinking, deciding; gets plaques.
  • Neuron parts: axon, dendrites, synapses, myelin (insulation), microtubules (inner train tracks held steady by tau).
  • Thyroid parafollicular cells: make calcitonin. Parathyroid glands: make PTH.
  • Kidney nephrons: fewer with age; renal mass shrinks.
  • Anal sphincters: lower resting and max pressure with age → fecal incontinence.
Physiology

Key definitions

  • Aging: slow wearing down of a mature body from time-based changes that cannot be undone.
  • Senescence: after-maturity changes that weaken balance (homeostasis) and raise vulnerability.
  • Life expectancy: age when 50% of a group is still alive. Life span: max length of life, fixed near 100 years.
  • Apoptosis: planned cell death. Necrosis: cell death from injury.

Theories of aging ★

GroupTheoryCore idea

Developmental (genes in charge)

Telomere / cellular senescence ()Telomeres shorten each cell division; when too short, the cell stops dividing (cellular senescence). (File text cut off here; Merck adds that most body cells lack enough telomerase to rebuild telomeres Ⓜ Merck.)
Immunologic theoryAging comes from weaker T cells and stronger autoimmune responses.
Antagonistic pleiotropyA gene helps you when young (reproduction) but harms you when old.
NeuroendocrineThe hypothalamic–pituitary axis (brain + hormones) drives aging. Example: menopause.

Stochastic (random damage piles up)

Free radical theoryReactive oxygen species (ROS) from mitochondria (electron transport chain) damage mitochondrial DNA; damage adds up.
Somatic mutation theoryPoor DNA repair, weak antioxidant defense, or protein errors cause aging.
Accumulated mutations (key term)Mutations that show up late in life escape natural selection, so they pile up. (Matches the file's line "mutations that occur later in life limit the influence of evolutionary selection".)

Caloric restriction extends life in experiments, maybe by shifting energy from growth toward repair.

Five common features of aging

  1. More deaths with age after maturity
  2. Tissue chemistry changes
  3. Steady drop in physical capacity
  4. Weaker ability to adapt to the environment
  5. More vulnerable to disease

System changes

Cells: atrophy and hypertrophy, poor mitosis, lipid deposits, free radical damage, waste buildup, lipofuscin (brown "wear-and-tear" pigment from lysosome digestion) → stiffening.

Fluids: less muscle, more fat → less total body water; kidneys handle sodium and water worse → hyponatremia (water held) or hypernatremia (water lost).

Calcium: PTH raises blood calcium (pulls from bone); calcitonin lowers it (puts into bone); active vitamin D raises gut absorption. With age: less gut absorption, blunted vitamin D activation, higher PTH (, 19.8). Calcium level changes with albumin and pH (less bound in acidosis, more bound in alkalosis).

Phosphate: usually lower. Magnesium: low is common. Acid–base: lungs and kidneys correct slower.

Thyroid: lower T3, higher TSH; hypothyroidism rises with age; hyperthyroidism does not.

Temperature: fewer, smaller, less active sweat glands → heat stress; poor cold sensing and shivering → hypothermia.

Heart and vessels: stiff arteries → higher afterload → can change cardiac output; LV wall thickens with hypertension; atherosclerosis from thicker intima.

Lungs: lower inspiratory reserve volume, higher residual volume, lower ventilatory capacity, lower V/Q ratio; weaker cough and mucus clearance + dysphagia → pneumonia and aspiration.

Muscle: sarcopenia (muscle loss) from inactivity, nerve changes, low protein; fast type II fibers lost faster than slow type I.

Kidneys: less blood flow, lower GFR, less mass, weaker proximal tubules, less renin and aldosterone → drug toxicity risk.

Gut: enteric nervous system changes; more rectal compliance + less rectal sensation → constipation; weaker sphincters → fecal incontinence; blunted hunger.

Nervous system: smaller brain, bigger ventricles, fewer neurons, dendrites and synapses, less myelin, altered neurotransmitters, slower conduction. Mild forgetfulness and smaller vocabulary usually not until the 70s.

Skin and hair: dry, wrinkled, patchy pigment, less elastic collagen; graying from low melanin.

Step-by-Step Processes

The sequences (reference)

K1. Amyloid plaque formation

  1. APP sits in the neuron membrane.
  2. Beta-secretase cuts APP.
  3. Gamma-secretase cuts again → amyloid beta fragment released.
  4. Fragments clump into oligomers (small clusters).
  5. Oligomers form fibrils (chains).
  6. Fibrils fold into beta-sheets.
  7. Senile plaques form outside neurons in hippocampus and cortex.

K2. Tau tangle to neuron death

  1. Tau holds microtubules steady.
  2. Tau turns "sticky" and tangles with other tau.
  3. Microtubules fall apart.
  4. Nutrients cannot move along the axon.
  5. Neuron stops working and cannot talk to others.
  6. Neuron dies; brain shrinks.

K3. Menopausal bone loss

  1. Ovarian estrogen falls.
  2. IL-6 rises.
  3. Osteoclasts live longer; osteoblasts die sooner.
  4. Resorption beats formation.
  5. Bone mass drops → osteopenia → osteoporosis → fracture.

K4. Alzheimer stages 1–7 ★

K5. Diagnosing osteoporosis

  1. Unexplained fracture or risk factors → suspect.
  2. Central DEXA of hip and spine.
  3. Read T score (normal / osteopenia / osteoporosis).
  4. Check Z score; less than −1.5 → look for secondary cause with labs.
  5. Repeat DEXA yearly or every 2 years.
Genetic Factors
  • Developmental theories: genes are the main driver of aging.
  • Telomeres shorten with each division ().
  • Antagonistic pleiotropy: one gene, good young, bad old.
  • Progeria: LMNA mutation → progerin (Merck for mechanism).
  • Alzheimer genes: APP, PS1, PS2; their faulty proteins are toxic to neurons → neuron death, synapse loss, tangles and plaques. About 20% of AD clusters in families. Down syndrome (extra chromosome 21, which carries APP Ⓜ Merck) raises AD risk.
  • Osteoporosis: genetic predisposition and first-degree family history; peak bone mass is partly genetic.
  • Cancer in aging: mutations pile up and DNA repair weakens.

Making Connections
  • Ch 13 (hormones and metabolism): PTH, calcitonin, vitamin D, thyroid changes all show up again here in calcium balance and osteoporosis.
  • Ch 14 (reproduction): menopause → loss of estrogen → menopausal bone loss via IL-6.
  • Ch 12 (sensory): aging adds sensory loss (taste, smell, vibration, vision, hearing) → falls and poor nutrition.
  • Immunity: immune senescence → more shingles, TB reactivation, pneumonia and flu deaths, hospital infections → vaccines matter.
  • Cancer: weak immunity + piled-up mutations + long carcinogen exposure + poor DNA repair → peak cancer age 65–80. Breast, lung cancer and multiple myeloma can cause dangerously high calcium.
  • Pharm: lower GFR + less body water + more fat → drugs last longer → toxicity.
  • Mental health: depression and anxiety are more common in long-term care and lower motivation to take meds, eat, exercise, and bathe.

Disease Cards
Osteoporosis ("brittle bone disease", "silent disease")
Patho (cell → symptom)
Bone is always being rebuilt. Osteoclasts break bone down. Osteoblasts build it. In osteoporosis, breakdown wins → bone mass drops → spongy (trabecular) bone loses its support beams and compact (cortical) bone gets thin and full of holes → bone breaks easily (). Spine has lots of spongy bone, so vertebral fractures are common.

Menopausal bone loss: low estrogen → more IL-6 (a cytokine, a signal protein) → osteoclasts live longer and osteoblasts die sooner → fast loss.

Senescent bone loss (both sexes, slower): fewer osteoblasts are made → slower bone building.

Etiology / types
Primary (aging, menopause). Secondary (less common): caused by another disease, hormone or genetic problem, or drug (); marked by poor osteoblast function. Merck examples of secondary causes: long-term corticosteroids, hyperparathyroidism, hyperthyroidism, low testosterone, chronic kidney or liver disease Ⓜ Merck.
Signs vs symptoms
Signs (you see/measure): loss of height, hyperkyphosis ("hunchback", ), fractures (vertebrae, hip, wrist), low T score.

Symptoms (patient feels): back pain, stiffness. Often NO symptoms until a fracture.

Diagnostics (test → finding → why)
★ Central DEXA (hip + lumbar spine) → T score → shows bone density; repeat yearly or every 2 years to track change.

★ Quantitative ultrasound (QUS) of heel → bone quality → cheap and fast but less accurate; heel may not match hip/spine.

Labs for secondary causes: CBC; chemistry (calcium, phosphorus, total protein, liver enzymes, alkaline phosphatase, electrolytes); urine calcium; hormones (TSH, vitamin D, PTH, cortisol).

Z score less than −1.5 → think secondary cause.

Risk factors + complications
Risks: genetics, first-degree relative with osteoporosis, low peak bone mass, BMI less than 25, loss of estrogen, aging, low calcium and vitamin D, smoking, too much alcohol, sedentary life. Females 8× more often.

Complications: hip and vertebral fractures, hyperkyphosis, falls, loss of independence.

Treatment
Short-term: fall-proof the home, pain control after fracture.

Long-term: ★ prevention first: calcium + vitamin D, weight-bearing exercise, no smoking, limit alcohol, bone density test, meds when needed.

Meds: antiresorptives (bisphosphonates alendronate and risedronate, calcitonin, estrogen, raloxifene) + anabolic teriparatide (D Drug Cards). Merck adds denosumab and zoledronic acid Ⓜ Merck.

Therapeutic: weight-bearing exercise (walking, stair climbing, dancing). Palliative: pain control and bracing after vertebral fracture.

Prognosis
Not given in this file. Ⓜ Merck hip fractures carry high illness and death risk in older adults, and treatment lowers fracture risk.
Independent nursing interventions
1. Fall-risk check + clear walkways, night lights, non-slip shoes, because one fall can mean a hip fracture.

2. Teach and encourage weight-bearing exercise, because loading the bone stimulates osteoblasts.

3. Teach calcium + vitamin D foods and sun exposure, because they are the bone's raw materials.

4. Measure height at each visit, because lost height is an early sign of vertebral collapse.
5. Gentle handling and log-rolling when moving frail patients, because bones can break with little force.

Alzheimer Disease (AD)
Patho (cell → symptom)
★ APP (amyloid precursor protein) sits in the neuron's membrane. Enzymes beta-secretase and gamma-secretase cut it → amyloid beta (Aβ) fragment → clumps grow: oligomers → fibrils → beta-sheets → senile plaques OUTSIDE neurons in hippocampus and cortex (, 19.10).

★ Tau normally holds microtubules steady like a backbone. In AD, tau gets "sticky" → neurofibrillary tangles INSIDE neurons → microtubules fall apart → no nutrient transport → neuron dies.

Tangles start in the temporal lobe → medial temporal lobe and hippocampus (memory) → spread over the cortex (executive, motor, sensory). Loss of the cholinergic (acetylcholine) system → memory failure, ADL loss, personality change. Dementia severity matches the number and location of tangles. Brain shrinks (atrophy, ).

Etiology
Genes + environment. Sporadic late-onset AD is most common; about 20% of cases cluster in families. Genes: APP, presenilin-1 (PS1), presenilin-2 (PS2).
Signs vs symptoms
Early symptoms: memory loss, confusion, restlessness, mood swings, trouble reading visual information. Signs (examiner sees): poor mental status scores, word-finding trouble, wandering, incontinence, impaired swallowing late. Stages 1–7 in section K.
Diagnostics
★ Definitive: autopsy (plaques + tangles seen). Supportive: full neuro exam, mental status exam (time and place, communication, memory, comprehension, simple math), reasoning, balance, visual-motor coordination. Brain scan, MRI, or CT to rule out other causes. Merck adds amyloid PET and CSF or blood biomarkers in selected patients Ⓜ Merck.
Risk + complications
Risks: older age, family history, obesity, dyslipidemia, hypertension, Down syndrome, traumatic brain injury, lead exposure, depression.

Complications: falls, wandering, aspiration (impaired swallowing), malnutrition, incontinence, caregiver strain.

Treatment
No cure. Supportive care for patient and family; quality of life is the cornerstone.

★ Cholinesterase inhibitors (ChEIs) replace lost acetylcholine effect. Others for secondary symptoms: antidepressants, antianxiety drugs, antipsychotics.

Ⓜ Merck memantine (NMDA antagonist) for moderate–severe, and anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies in selected patients.

Palliative: late-stage comfort, feeding and swallowing support.

Prognosis
Long, progressive course with eventual complete loss of function. Ⓜ Merck survival after diagnosis often about 7 years, varies widely.
Independent nursing interventions
1. Keep a calm, structured routine, because change raises confusion and agitation.

2. Safety: door alarms, ID bracelet, remove hazards, because wandering and poor judgment cause injury.

3. Short, simple sentences, one step at a time, because processing is slow.

4. Reorient gently with clocks, calendars, photos, because time and place fade first.

5. Swallow checks, upright for meals, because late AD impairs swallowing (aspiration risk).
6. Support and teach caregivers, because burnout is common.

Progeria (Hutchinson-Gilford Progeria Syndrome, HGPS)
Patho
A rare genetic disease where children age very fast. Ⓜ Merck a new (de novo) point mutation in the LMNA gene makes progerin, a faulty form of lamin A (a nuclear support protein). Progerin builds up and damages the cell nucleus → early aging of tissues, especially blood vessels Ⓜ Merck. The file's key terms list "progerin" and the treatment section mentions "mutant LMNA".
Etiology
Genetic (LMNA). Ⓜ Merck usually not inherited from parents; it is a new mutation Ⓜ Merck.
Signs vs symptoms
Signs (): prominent scalp veins, scleroderma (shiny tight skin) and wrinkled skin, loss of fat under skin and muscle, delayed teeth, hearing loss, high-pitched voice, short stature and skeletal dysplasia, incomplete sexual maturation. Symptom: feeding difficulties.

Later: osteoporosis, atherosclerosis, fibrosis, hypertension, arthritis.

★ NOT seen: more tumors, cataracts, or senility.

Diagnostics
History + physical. ★ Hyaluronic acid levels give clues (high). Imaging for bone and vessel damage; special heart tests. Ⓜ Merck genetic testing confirms the LMNA mutation.
Risk + complications
★ Death most often from atherosclerosis (heart attack, stroke).
Treatment
File: "There is no approved therapy for progeria." Research: gene therapy (stop mutant LMNA) and rapamycin. Supportive: feeding and nutrition, activity and joint mobility, vascular monitoring, family support.

Merck says: lonafarnib, an oral farnesyltransferase inhibitor, is FDA-approved and lowers progerin build-up and death rates Ⓜ Merck. (Your file is older here; answer by your textbook on the exam.)

Prognosis
Not given in this file. Ⓜ Merck average life expectancy is about 14.5 years Ⓜ Merck.
Independent nursing interventions
1. Small frequent high-calorie meals, because feeding is hard and fat stores are low.

2. Range-of-motion and activity, because joints stiffen.

3. Watch for chest pain or stroke signs, because atherosclerosis is the killer.
4. Family emotional support, because the course is short and hard.

Med Math (practice)

Answers are blurred. Decide first, then tap to check.

Problems 1–2 use the file's real calcium and vitamin D numbers. Problems 3–5 are clearly labeled PRACTICE doses, not orders to follow.

  1. Calcium split. Goal 1,200 mg/day in 2 equal doses. Each dose? 1,200 ÷ 2 = 600 mg.
  2. Vitamin D tablets. Goal 600 IU/day; tablets are 200 IU. 600 ÷ 200 = 3 tablets.
  3. PRACTICE: tablets. Order 70 mg; tablets 35 mg. 70 ÷ 35 = 2 tablets.
  4. PRACTICE: liquid. Order 10 mg; supply 5 mg/5 mL. (10 ÷ 5) × 5 = 10 mL.
  5. PRACTICE: IV rate. 500 mL over 4 hours. 500 ÷ 4 = 125 mL/hr.

Unfolding Case Study

Patient: Mrs. Rosa L., 74, lives alone, postmenopausal, smoked 30 years, BMI 21.

Stage 1 (clinic): She says she is "shrinking" (5 cm, or 2 in, shorter than her license says) and has mild back ache. Questions: What disease do you suspect? What test first? Answer: Osteoporosis (height loss + back pain + risks: female, smoker, BMI less than 25, age). Central DEXA of hip and spine.

Stage 2 (results): T score −2.9 at the hip; Z score −0.8. Questions: Name the result. Primary or secondary? Answer: Osteoporosis (below −2.5). Z score is not below −1.5, so likely primary. Start calcium 1,200 mg/day divided, vitamin D, weight-bearing exercise, quit smoking; provider orders a bisphosphonate.

Stage 3 (6 months later, ED): She trips on a rug at night and has a shortened, outwardly rotated right leg. Her daughter adds: "Mom keeps forgetting to take her pills and got lost driving to church." Questions: Priority? What else must be evaluated? Answer: Suspected hip fracture: immobilize, pain control, neurovascular checks. New memory loss + getting lost → screen cognition (mental status exam), imaging to rule out other causes; possible early Alzheimer (stage 3–4).

Stage 4 (discharge planning): After hip repair, she scores low on a mental status exam. Questions: What teaching and safety plan? Answer: Home safety (remove rugs, night lights, grab bars), pill organizer and family supervision of meds, bisphosphonate rules (empty stomach, water, upright 30 minutes Ⓜ Merck), PT for weight-bearing, caregiver support, ID bracelet, follow-up for cholinesterase inhibitor discussion.

Teaching Scripts + Teach-Back

Script 1: Bone health. "Your bones are like a bank. After 50 we take out more than we put in. Each day, take your calcium in two smaller doses so your body absorbs more. Get vitamin D. Walk, climb stairs, or dance most days, because putting weight on bones tells them to get stronger. Stop smoking and limit alcohol." Teach-back: "Tell me two things you will do this week for your bones."

Script 2: Taking a bisphosphonate Ⓜ Merck. "Take it first thing in the morning on an empty stomach with a full glass of plain water. Stay sitting or standing for at least 30 minutes, and wait before eating. Lying down can let it burn your food pipe." Teach-back: "Show me how you will take your pill tomorrow morning."

Script 3: Home safety. "Remove loose rugs, use night lights, keep a phone near, and wear shoes with good grip." Teach-back: "Walk me through your path to the bathroom at night."

Script 4: Caregiver of a person with AD. "Keep the day the same each day. Use short sentences. Lock doors and add an ID bracelet in case of wandering. Take breaks; caring for yourself is part of caring for them." Teach-back: "What will you do if your dad tries to leave the house at night?"

Script 5: Heat and cold. "Older bodies sweat less and don't sense cold well. In heat, drink fluids and stay in cool rooms. In cold, dress in layers." Teach-back: "What are two signs you are getting too hot?"

Safety / Infection Control
  • Falls: bed low, call light near, non-slip socks, clear paths, night lights, assist with walking.
  • Fragile bones: lift with a sheet, never pull on limbs.
  • Wandering (AD): door alarms, ID bracelet, frequent checks.
  • Aspiration: upright for meals, swallow screen, thickened liquids if ordered.
  • Infection: hand hygiene, flu and pneumonia vaccines, shingles vaccine Ⓜ Merck, TB screening (TB reactivation rises with age). Older adults have more hospital-acquired infections.
  • Fever may be low or absent in older adults with infection Ⓜ Merck; new confusion can be the only sign.
  • Meds: lower GFR → toxicity; check kidney function, use pill organizers, supervise if memory loss.
  • Skin: turn every 2 hours, pad bony areas (thin skin and poor healing).
  • Temperature: watch for heat stress and hypothermia.

Cultural / Ethical
  • Consent: cancer treatment in older adults depends on type, effects, age, quality of life, and consent (file). In AD, decision-making ability fades: plan advance directives and a health care proxy early Ⓜ Merck.
  • Ageism: don't brush off pain or confusion as "just old age."
  • Family roles: many cultures expect family to care for elders at home; respect this and offer support.
  • Geography: where people live changes sun exposure and vitamin D (file Stop and Consider).
  • Diet and culture: plan calcium sources that fit the person's food culture (dairy, greens, fortified foods, tofu).
  • Ethics in progeria: research and gene therapy questions; family support and child-centered care.
  • Restraints in AD: avoid when possible; least restrictive measure first.

Hey Josh, welcome to Chapter 19: Degenerative Changes in Aging. Let's make this stick.

First, why do we age? Two camps. The developmental camp says it's in your genes. Think of telomeres, the little caps on your chromosomes, like the plastic tips on shoelaces. Every time a cell divides, the tip wears down. When it's gone, the cell retires. Other developmental ideas: the immune system weakens, some genes help you young but hurt you old, and the brain-hormone axis changes, like in menopause.

The stochastic camp says it's random damage piling up. Free radicals from your mitochondria nick your DNA. DNA repair gets sloppy. Over decades, it adds up.

Now, what does aging look like? Less muscle, more fat, so less body water. That means sodium swings and dehydration hit harder. The immune system gets weaker at fighting germs but more likely to attack itself. The thymus shrinks to 15 percent of its peak after 50. Shingles and TB come back. Wounds heal slower because skin is thin and capillaries are weak.

The brain loses neurons and myelin, nerve signals slow down, and a brown pigment called lipofuscin builds up. Mild forgetfulness is normal, usually after 70. Losing your way home is not.

Bones: osteoclasts are the demolition crew, osteoblasts are the builders. After menopause, low estrogen raises IL-6, the demolition crew works longer, and the builders quit early. That's fast menopausal bone loss. Then everyone gets slow senescent bone loss.

Osteoporosis is the silent disease. No symptoms until a fracture, a hunched spine, or lost height. Test with a central DEXA scan of the hip and spine. Remember the T score song: above minus one, fine. Minus one to minus two and a half, osteopenia. Below minus two and a half, osteoporosis. A Z score below minus one point five means look for another cause.

Treatment: prevention first. Calcium 1,200 milligrams a day, split up. Vitamin D 400 to 600 units. Weight-bearing exercise. No smoking, less alcohol. Drugs mostly slow breakdown: bisphosphonates, calcitonin, estrogen, raloxifene. One drug builds bone: teriparatide.

Alzheimer disease: plaques out, tangles in. Amyloid beta, cut from APP by beta and gamma secretase, clumps outside neurons into plaques. Tau gets sticky inside neurons, knots up the microtubule tracks, and the neuron starves and dies. The hippocampus, your memory center, gets hit early. Tangles track with how bad the dementia is. Only autopsy proves it. Treatment is supportive, plus cholinesterase inhibitors to boost acetylcholine.

Finally, progeria: kids who age super fast. Watch for scalp veins, tight shiny skin, short height, a high voice. The lab clue is high hyaluronic acid. They usually die from atherosclerosis. And no extra tumors, cataracts, or senility.

Quick recall before you go: Plaques? Outside. Tangles? Inside. Osteoporosis T score? Below minus two and a half. Progeria killer? Atherosclerosis. You've got this.

Memory Aids (your story style)
  • 🦷 "Osteo-CLASTS = CLear Away; osteo-BLASTS = Build." Clasts are the demolition crew, blasts are the builders. In osteoporosis, the demolition crew works overtime.
